# Why international government and international industry are one track

You asked whether international industry should be mixed in with U.S. commercial
industry. The answer this dataset uses is **no — and the reason also explains why
international government and international industry are not split from each other.**

## The cleave is regulatory regime, not buyer type

Sorting buyers by whether they are public or private feels natural and is wrong here,
because it cuts across the constraint that actually decides whether a sale can happen.
Three genuinely different gates exist:

| Track | Gate that binds | Who authorises |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. Government | DoD/DOE authorisation pathway; the Army is standing up **Army-regulated** reactors, and DOE authorised the DOME experiments outside NRC licensing entirely | Service branch, DIU, DOE |
| U.S. Commercial | NRC licence **plus** utility interconnection and state PUC process | NRC + state PUC |
| International | U.S. export control — 10 CFR Part 810, NRC export licensing, a 123 Agreement with the host country — **plus** the host regulator | DOE/NNSA, NRC, and e.g. UK ONR or CNSC |

A U.S. federal buyer can route around the NRC in a way no commercial buyer can. That is
not a small procedural difference; it is the single biggest reason the government track
is the only one with a named reactor paired to a named site today.

## Why international government and industry belong together

For a U.S. microreactor vendor, the binding constraint on **any** foreign sale is the
export-control stack. That stack does not care whether the counterparty is a ministry or
a port operator: DP World at Thames Freeport and the Saskatchewan Research Council both
sit behind the same U.S. authorisation before either can take delivery. Splitting them
would create two tracks that share a gate and differ only in the counterparty's
ownership — a distinction that changes the sales motion but not the feasibility.

## Why international industry must not merge into U.S. commercial

The merge would put **DP World Thames Freeport** next to **Equinix** as though they were
the same sale. They are not:

- Equinix needs an NRC licence and a U.S. interconnection.
- DP World needs a UK ONR design assessment *and* a U.S. export authorisation.

Same sector (logistics/data infrastructure), same rough scale, completely different
deal. A segmentation that hides that is a segmentation that will mis-forecast.

## The honest caveat

This cleave is clean for feasibility and messy for sales coverage: a global industrial
like ExxonMobil or Rio Tinto appears in the U.S. commercial track via the IANC while its
non-U.S. sites fall in the international track. The dataset keeps those as one row under
the consortium rather than duplicating the account, and flags it here rather than
pretending the boundary is crisp. If the pipeline is ever used for territory assignment
rather than feasibility, this is the first thing that will need re-cutting.
